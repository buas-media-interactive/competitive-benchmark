INTD / Creative benchmark

Creative benchmark, step by step.

In this exercise you look closely at the brands your client competes with, so that your campaign starts from what is already out there. You build it on one FigJam board, in three parts, and you come back to the same board as the weeks go by. You hand in the benchmark together with your Creative Brief at the end of Week 3.

Before you start.

  • One board. Duplicate the template into your team’s FigJam. This guide explains each step, and the board is where you do the work.
  • A board that grows. You will come back to the same cards in later weeks. Each time you do, add a line to the cover frame with what you added and what made you change your mind. That is also useful evidence for your oral exam.
  • Who does what. In Part 1, each of you takes one or two competitors. Put your name and your sticky colour on every card, so it is clear who did what. In Part 2 you work as a team, except for the reverse-engineering step, which you each do on your own.
  • Evidence. Add a link or a screenshot, and the date you saw it, to everything you write down. Campaigns change quickly, and anyone reading your board needs to be able to check.
  • What you already know. Every step uses something we have covered in a lecture or workshop. If a term is new, we explain it in a line.
  • Culture. Use the identity, practice and brand lenses from the kick-off, and base what you say on what the brand shows and says. Please don’t infer a layer from how someone looks, their name or their nationality.
  • The examples. They use oat drinks (Oatly, Alpro and dairy milk), which is not your client’s category. They come from press coverage, so look at the real pieces yourself, and please don’t reuse the example answers.

Part 1 / Week 1

Scan and read.

Here you find out who else is in this space, what they offer and how they sound. Then you read one piece of their advertising closely.

You can start steps 1 to 4 right away. Steps 5 and 6 use Lecture 2 and Workshop 2, so leave them until after Wednesday. Aim for about two hours each. This is a first look, not a full analysis.

Choose your competitors.Three to five brands, each with a reason.

Pick three to five brands your audience might choose instead of your client, or alongside it. Try to include at least one direct and one indirect competitor.

  • Direct: meets the same need in the same way.
  • Indirect: meets the same need in a different way.

For each brand, write one sentence on why it is comparable. Think about the brands your audience actually comes across, and not only the ones you already know. Then agree who in your team takes which brand, and add the competitors to your stakeholder map, since they are stakeholders too.

Example, competitor set for an oat drink

Alpro, direct: another plant-based drink on the same shelf.

Dairy milk, indirect: the same need (milk for coffee or cereal), met in a different way.

Compare what they offer.Products or services, prices and where they are sold.

For each competitor, note the main products or services, what they cost and where people can buy them. Give every price a source and a date, for example a shelf price, a website or a menu.

Please compare like with like. Use the same unit for every brand, such as price per litre, per month or per visit, depending on your category.

Example, the format of a row

Alpro: plant-based drinks, plus yoghurt-style products. Price: € per litre, shop, date seen. Sold in: supermarkets.

Fill in your own prices. This example only shows the format, not real figures.

Log the pieces.At least three recent pieces per brand.

For each competitor, collect at least three recent pieces from different places, such as ads, packaging, social posts, street or in-store activity and press. For each piece, note where it ran, the format, who is speaking, a link or screenshot, and the date you saw it.

  • Format: use the media types from Lecture 1, such as out-of-home, print, video, radio, social or pack.
  • Who is speaking: the brand itself, or someone else talking about the brand, such as the press, creators or customers.

Once you have the pieces, ask yourself how often and how consistently the brand shows up.

Example, Oatly

  • Pack: a climate message printed on cartons, 2026. The brand itself.
  • Street and café: outdoor ads and a free-coffee tour in the UK, 2025. The brand itself.
  • Place: a bike-thru café in Amsterdam, 2026. The brand itself, and press coverage by others.
  • Presence: campaigns in at least four markets (UK, Netherlands, Sweden, Spain), with one voice and different topics.
Describe tone and message.Tone and message for each brand.

Describe the tone in three words, and give each word a short piece of evidence from the piece, such as a phrase or a visual detail. Then write the message in one sentence: what is this piece telling people?

Do this for each piece first, and then summarise it per brand. If a brand’s pieces say different things, write that down, because it tells you something about the brand.

Weak

Friendly, fun and modern.

Strong

Dry, ironic, direct. The carton copy feigns politeness to politicians, then undercuts it with “no, of course not.”

Example, Oatly’s message, per piece

2025 outdoor: the taste is better than you expect (blind taste test).

2026 carton: most people want climate action, so politicians should act.

Read one piece closely.Two or three signs per brand, with denotation and connotation.

Choose one piece per brand and use the first steps of the five-step method from Lecture 2 and Workshop 2: observe first, then analyse and deconstruct. Pick out two or three signs, such as a colour, a typeface, a setting, a person, an object or a word. For each sign, write what it literally shows (denotation) and what it suggests (connotation).

Weak

The carton has a lot of text. It feels bold and different.

Strong, Oatly carton

Denotation: two text blocks run down the side, one of 11 words and one of 89.

Connotation: the block lengths make a statistic visible, like a bar chart made of words. Opening with “Politicians” makes the shopper a witness, not the target.

Look at who is shown and who is missing.Shown, spoken to and missing, with one or two layers.

Stay with the same piece. Ask who is shown, who is spoken to and who is missing. Then name one or two layers from the identity and practice lenses that the piece draws on.

  • Identity: generation, ethnicity or migration background, religion or worldview, language, gender and sexuality, class, region.
  • Practice: rituals, values, taste and media habits, platform culture, subcultures, professional cultures.

Only use what the piece shows or says. If you can, add a hypothesis about whom the piece could misfire for, and why. Label it as a hypothesis, since you have not tested it.

Example, Oatly carton

Whose story: citizens as a silent majority, speaking to politicians. The minority appears only as the 11-word block.

Layers: values (civic voice), taste and media habits (a brand as a political voice).

Possible misfire: people who read a political message on a food pack as preaching. This is something to test, not to assume.

Part 2 / Week 2

Compare and decide.

Now you put your competitors side by side, ask what each one is doing for its audience, and decide what that means for your own campaign.

Start with Part 1, because Part 2 builds on your Week 1 board and on this week’s lectures on brands, objectives and people. Steps 9 and 10 fit well inside the workshops.

Hand-in: you submit the benchmark together with your Creative Brief at the end of Week 3.

Add a criterion of your own.One question, with a reason, applied to every brand.

Now it is your turn to decide what is worth comparing. Add one criterion and apply it to every competitor. There are two good places to look:

  • Your client’s challenge. Is there something in it that you would want to compare across brands?
  • Part 1. Did you notice something that none of the criteria captured?

A good criterion is something you can observe in the pieces rather than guess. It matters in your client’s category, and it adds something the other criteria don’t. A simple test: would your synthesis lose something if you dropped it?

Write it as a question in the template header, add one line on why you chose it, and apply it to the pieces you already collected. It will become a column in your synthesis in step 11. If you are stuck, you could look at format habits in the category, how a brand handles criticism, or what it chooses not to say. Try your own idea first.

Example, a criterion noticed in Part 1

Question: how does the brand handle criticism?

Observed: Oatly’s Spanish campaign invites people to learn about the company through the bad things said about it, including a staged comedy roast.

Why it matters here: it shows whether a brand in this category can laugh at itself.

Example, a criterion from a challenge (made up)

If a client’s challenge is awareness among newcomers, a criterion could be: how easily can a stranger understand what this brand is from a single piece?

Look at each brand as a sender.Personality, associations and a positioning sentence.

Now look at each competitor as a sender, using the brand lens from the kick-off. For each brand, write down:

  • Personality: three words, as we did in the lecture on brands.
  • Associations: what the brand makes people think of, backed by pieces.
  • Category conventions: what it does that other brands in the category also do.
  • Positioning: one sentence on who it is for and how it differs from the others.

Example, Oatly (our reading)

Personality: dry, outspoken, irreverent.

Positioning: for people who want a plant-based drink and are comfortable with a brand that argues in public. It argues where Alpro describes flavour.

Map think, feel and do.What each piece wants its audience to think, feel and do.

For the piece you read in Part 1, write one line for each of these:

  • Think: what should people know or believe afterwards?
  • Feel: what should they feel?
  • Do: what should they do next? If there is no clear ask, say so.

Then say which of the three matters most for that piece.

Example, Oatly carton

Think: most people want climate action.

Feel: part of a majority.

Do: check the real carton. Is there a clear ask, or is the “do” simply to keep buying?

Reverse-engineer a campaign.Each person takes one competitor campaign: message, big idea, concept and insight.

This is your Workshop 3 homework, and you each do it on your own, using a competitor’s campaign. We want you to look at a lot of different campaigns, so please choose a different one from your teammates. Use the worksheet from the workshop and work backwards from what you see to:

  • the message;
  • the big idea;
  • the concept;
  • the consumer insight it seems to rest on.

You cannot know what the agency knew, so mark the insight as your own reading, something you would still need to test. Put your campaign on the board with your name on it.

Example, Oatly carton (our reading)

Message: most people want climate action, so politicians should act.

Big idea: let the carton speak to politicians.

Insight it seems to rest on: shoppers who already agree want a way to be counted.

Bring it together and decide.One team frame, with conclusions for your Creative Brief.

Now you move from describing to deciding. Work as a team on one frame with three parts:

  • Part A: one row per competitor, with look and tone, message and positioning, offer and price, channels, and your own criterion.
  • Part B: three headings. Conventions: what most of the set does. Follow or break: what your agency will do with each convention, and why. Open space: what competitors leave open.
  • Conclusions: a short paragraph of three to five sentences on what the benchmark showed, what it means for your campaign, and which question of your brief it feeds. Say what is evidence and what is your own reading.

Every entry should point to at least one piece on your board. Question 1.3 of your brief asks for exactly this: which conventions exist, which ones you will follow or deliberately break, and what is left open. Before you submit, duplicate the frame and label the copy “Submitted with brief”, so you can keep working on the original.

Check before you finish.

  • Each convention points to a piece on your board.
  • Each convention has a clear follow or break, with a reason.
  • Open space says where, not “be more creative”.
  • Your own criterion appears in the table and changes at least one decision.
  • Your conclusions name the brief question they feed.

Part 3 / Weeks 4 and 5

Return and sharpen.

In Weeks 4 and 5 you come back to the same cards with new tools. This part opens in Week 4.

Label the channels.Coming soonA paid, owned, earned or shared label for every piece in your log.

After the lecture on media planning, go back to your Part 1 log and give each piece a label.

  • Paid: bought space. Owned: the brand’s own pack, site, accounts or venue.
  • Earned: others talk about it, as in press and reviews. Shared: people post it or pass it on.

Then ask where your competitors are absent, and add one line to the cover frame about what you changed.

Example, Oatly

Carton: owned. Outdoor ads: paid. Press coverage of the Bike Thru café: earned.
